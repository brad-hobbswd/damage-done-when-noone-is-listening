Part 2 · Chapter 5

A Person's Absence Is Not Permission

✧

Remembering the dignity of absent people while distinguishing gossip from responsible reporting.

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scriptures: Proverbs 11:13; Proverbs 16:28; Matthew 18:15–17; Ephesians 4:15, 25, 29; Galatians 6:1

Matthew 18:15–17

Moreover if thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone: if he shall hear thee, thou hast gained thy brother. But if he will not hear thee, then take with thee one or two more, that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established. And if he shall neglect to hear them, tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican.

Absence Does Not Remove Dignity

It is easier to speak carelessly about someone when they are not in the room. We do not have to see their face, hear their response, or notice the moment our words change how they are regarded. Their absence can make a serious matter feel like ordinary conversation. Yet a person's dignity does not disappear when they leave, and their inability to answer does not give us permission to speak without restraint.

Ephesians gives believers a practical standard for speech:

Ephesians 4:29

Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.

That instruction asks more of us than avoiding insults. It asks whether our words are truthful, necessary, and directed toward a constructive purpose. We may have legitimate concerns about someone's choices or conduct. We may need advice, help resolving a conflict, or a responsible way to report something serious. The question is not whether we may ever speak about an absent person. The question is why we are speaking, what we know, whom we are telling, and what responsible outcome we are seeking.

A person's absence is not permission to damage their dignity, and concern for their dignity is not a command to hide the truth.

When Conversation Becomes Gossip

Gossip often grows where curiosity, frustration, and the desire for agreement are allowed to lead. A conversation may begin with a genuine concern but shift toward speculation about motives, unnecessary private details, or repeated criticism that offers no path toward resolution. The absent person becomes a subject to discuss rather than a person whose dignity still matters.

Proverbs 16:28 warns that a whisperer separates close friends. Destructive speech does not merely transfer information. It can change relationships, encourage suspicion, and invite people to choose sides before they understand what happened. Even a statement that contains some truth can be used unfairly when shared to embarrass, punish, entertain, or recruit others into resentment.

Ask whether the conversation is moving toward a legitimate next step. Are you seeking wise counsel from someone who can help? Are you trying to understand what happened so you can respond fairly? Or are you repeating the story because you want others to share your opinion? If the conversation depends on speculation or the pleasure of diminishing someone, it needs to stop or change direction.

It is also important to separate what we observed from what we inferred. “I heard these words” is not the same as “I know the person's heart.” “This decision caused harm” is not the same as “Everything that person does is malicious.” We can name specific conduct without pretending to know every motive behind it.

Responsible Counsel Is Not the Same as Gossip

Some people have been taught that speaking about a concern is always disloyal or that every problem should be kept within the church. That is not a safe or responsible principle. There are times when a person needs counsel, support, protection, or an appropriate investigation. Seeking help from someone qualified to respond is not the same as spreading a story for personal satisfaction.

Responsible counsel is purposeful and proportionate. We share the information needed for the person to understand the concern and help determine an appropriate response. We avoid embellishment, distinguish firsthand knowledge from what others have told us, and acknowledge uncertainty. We do not recruit an audience when what we need is guidance or action.

Matthew 18:15–17 describes a process for addressing certain conflicts, but it must not be misused as a blanket requirement to confront an unsafe person alone or to handle abuse privately. When threats, abuse, exploitation, or serious misconduct may be occurring, safety comes first. Depending on the circumstances, responsible action may require a safeguarding lead, supervisor, qualified professional, or civil authority. Immediate safety needs and applicable reporting duties must be taken seriously.

Confidentiality should be respected, but it must not become an absolute promise to conceal harm. The goal is to share information with the right people for a legitimate reason, not to spread it widely or suppress it completely.

Truth Must Be Handled With Care

Speaking truthfully does not mean saying everything we know to everyone who will listen. A detail may be accurate but unnecessary to the matter at hand. A private disclosure may be relevant to a counselor or investigator but inappropriate for a casual group conversation. Wisdom considers both whether something is true and whether sharing it with this person, in this setting, serves a responsible purpose.

Ephesians 4:15 calls believers to speak the truth in love. Love does not require us to soften facts until they become misleading, and truth does not give us permission to humiliate people. A truthful response can be direct without being cruel, firm without being contemptuous, and careful without becoming evasive.

When discussing another person's conduct, describe the behavior as specifically and fairly as possible. Avoid labels that reduce a whole person to one incident. Do not add details to make the story more compelling. If new information changes your understanding, be willing to correct what you said before. Honesty includes the humility to revise a conclusion.

Do Not Use Discernment to Excuse Contempt

Believers sometimes use spiritual language to make personal resentment sound righteous. They may describe assumptions as discernment, curiosity as concern, or criticism as accountability. But the vocabulary we use does not determine the character of our actions. A conversation does not become godly merely because it includes prayer, a Bible verse, or a claim that we are trying to help.

Galatians 6:1 calls believers to restore someone who has fallen with a spirit of meekness, while considering themselves. The passage joins correction with humility. It does not encourage us to enjoy another person's failure or treat ourselves as incapable of needing mercy.

Before discussing someone's conduct, ask whether you are willing to help them move toward truth and restoration, or whether you mainly want others to share your negative opinion. Ask whether you have examined your own part in the conflict. These questions cannot resolve every difficult situation, but they can expose when our motives have drifted from responsibility toward contempt.

Restoration may not be immediate, a relationship may not be safe to continue, and formal accountability may be necessary. Meekness does not mean passivity, and forgiveness does not remove the need for boundaries or consequences. It means that even when we must speak firmly, we refuse to turn another person's struggle into an opportunity to exalt ourselves.

Choose the Right Audience and the Right Action

Before sharing a concern, identify its purpose. If you need emotional support, choose a trustworthy person who can listen without encouraging retaliation or spreading private details. If you need help resolving a disagreement, consider a direct conversation when safe and appropriate. If someone may be at risk, contact the people or services equipped to protect them. If you witnessed misconduct, report what you know through a suitable channel rather than turning the report into general conversation.

Share only what is needed for that purpose. Keep facts, interpretations, and unanswered questions distinct. Avoid promises of secrecy when someone may be in danger or a concern needs to be reported. If you are unsure how to proceed, seek guidance from a qualified professional or responsible authority.

If you discover that you have shared more than necessary, stop the spread and take responsibility. You may need to ask others not to repeat the information, correct a false impression, or acknowledge that you spoke beyond what you knew. Do not use the possibility of gossip as an excuse to withdraw a legitimate report. Make sure the concern reaches the appropriate people and is handled responsibly.

Let Dignity and Accountability Stand Together

We are not asked to choose between protecting people's dignity and telling the truth. Christian maturity requires both. Gossip treats information as something to pass around without regard for its effects. Responsible speech considers truth, purpose, audience, safety, and the next action needed.

Part One challenged us to examine our hearts, our judgments, and our integrity. This chapter brings those lessons into our conversations about people who are not present. Their absence does not lessen our responsibility before God, and it does not lessen our responsibility toward them. We must not use silence to conceal wrongdoing, and we must not use truth as a cover for needless harm.

Speak to bring truth into the light and to seek a responsible response, not to make another person's absence an opportunity for careless judgment.

Personal Application

Think of a recent conversation about someone who was not present. What was its purpose? Which statements were based on direct knowledge, and which involved assumptions or unnecessary details? Did the conversation lead toward a constructive response, or did it mainly encourage others to judge the person?

Choose one practical change. You may decide to stop repeating a private detail, speak directly and respectfully when safe, seek appropriate counsel, or report a serious concern through a responsible channel. If you have spread an unfair impression, consider how to correct it with the people who heard it. Do not use this reflection to silence yourself or someone else when real harm requires attention.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, teach me to honor the dignity of people whether they are present or absent. Guard me from gossip, contempt, and the desire to recruit others into my frustration. Give me wisdom to know when to remain silent, when to seek counsel, and when to speak or report a concern responsibly. Help me distinguish facts from assumptions and truth from unnecessary exposure. Make me humble enough to receive correction and courageous enough to address wrongdoing without cruelty. Let my words serve truth, protection, and restoration. In the name of Jesus, amen.

Questions for Reflection

  1. What changes in your speech when the person being discussed is not present?
  2. How can you recognize when a conversation has shifted from seeking counsel to spreading gossip?
  3. Why is responsible reporting different from repeating a story to an unnecessary audience?
  4. How can you speak truthfully without using truth to humiliate someone?
  5. What is one way you can protect another person's dignity while still addressing genuine harm?
