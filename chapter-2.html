Part One · Chapter Two

The Measure We Use on Others

✧

Before we judge another person's failure, Scripture calls us to examine the standard, spirit, and assumptions guiding our own judgment.

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scriptures: Matthew 7:1–5; Luke 6:37–38; Romans 2:1–4; James 4:11–12 (KJV)

The Danger of Becoming Our Own Standard

It is easy to recognize a fault in another person and much harder to recognize the same tendency when it appears in our own lives. We may see impatience in someone else and call it a character problem, while describing our own impatience as the result of stress. We may call another person's sharp words disrespectful, yet excuse our own because we were frustrated. We treat someone else's mistake as evidence of who they are, while asking others to judge us by everything we meant to do right.

This uneven measure should concern every believer. When we make ourselves the standard by which everyone else is evaluated, our judgment becomes distorted. We give ourselves context and give others a label. We ask people to understand our intentions while assuming we already understand theirs. We demand accountability from others while explaining away the behavior for which we are responsible.

Jesus' teaching in Matthew 7 does not require us to pretend right and wrong are indistinguishable. It confronts the hypocrisy of condemning others while refusing honest examination of ourselves. His image of the beam and the speck exposes the absurdity of trying to correct another person's vision while ignoring what obstructs our own. The question is not whether we will ever notice another person's failure. The question is whether we are willing to stand under the same light we want directed toward them.

Correction Begins With Honest Self Examination

Jesus says to remove the beam from our own eye before attempting to help our brother with the speck in his. The order matters. He does not say that the speck is imaginary or that correction has no place. He teaches that our own condition must be addressed before we assume the role of someone who can see clearly enough to correct another.

Self examination changes the spirit in which we approach a difficult matter. Instead of entering a conversation convinced that we are the righteous party and the other person is the problem, we become willing to ask what we may have misunderstood, contributed, or failed to address. We can still speak truth, but we are less likely to confuse truth with the satisfaction of proving someone wrong.

This is not self hatred, nor is it an invitation to accept blame for another person's choices or deny harm that has been done. It is the willingness to take responsibility for our own actions without using another person's wrongdoing to distract from them. A person can be genuinely mistreated and still need God to examine the way they respond. A leader can be wrong and still deserve a truthful account rather than an exaggerated one. Both realities can exist at the same time.

When we are honest about our weaknesses, we become more careful with the weaknesses of others. Humility does not remove accountability. It changes the way we carry it.

We Cannot Read Every Motive

One damaging habit in conflict is treating our interpretation of another person's motive as if it were established fact. We see an action and decide the person intended to insult us. We hear a brief response and conclude that someone is arrogant. We observe a decision without knowing all the circumstances and declare that we understand exactly why it was made.

Sometimes our interpretation may be right. Sometimes it may be incomplete. Our own history, disappointment, or fear can fill the gaps in what we know. Wisdom requires us to distinguish between what we directly observed and what we inferred. “That statement hurt me” describes an experience. “You said it because you wanted to destroy me” claims knowledge of a motive that may not have been established.

God knows the heart perfectly. We do not. This should make us careful, not passive. We can identify harmful conduct, ask questions, set boundaries, and report serious concerns without pretending to know every hidden intention. We can describe what happened and seek a responsible response while leaving room for facts we do not yet possess.

When we turn assumptions into accusations, we close the door to understanding before the other person has a fair opportunity to answer. We then repeat our conclusion to others, and each retelling can make our interpretation feel more certain. The result is not discernment but a verdict built on information we may never have had.

When Discernment Becomes a Disguise for Contempt

Believers rightly value discernment. Scripture calls us to test what we hear, recognize fruit, reject deception, and protect people from harm. Yet spiritual language can be used to protect an unspiritual attitude. We may call someone dangerous when what we mean is that we dislike them. We may describe a personal offense as a warning from God. We may claim to discern a person's spirit when we are actually repeating an untested suspicion.

True discernment does not give us permission to abandon truthfulness, fairness, or love. A concern should be weighed by what can be established, the seriousness of the matter, the people responsible for addressing it, and the steps needed to protect those affected. Serious allegations require care, accuracy, and an appropriate process.

There are times when immediate disclosure to responsible authorities is necessary, especially when abuse, exploitation, threats, or other serious harm may be occurring. Self examination must never become a tool for silencing people who need protection. But neither should the language of protection justify sharing details with people who cannot help and have no legitimate need to know.

We should seek to address what needs to change, not to humiliate a person. We should want a harmful situation stopped, not simply enjoy having a story that places us above someone else.

The Measure We Give Returns to Our Own Lives

Jesus warns against judging by a standard we refuse to accept ourselves. His words call us to remember that we, too, depend upon mercy, correction, and the patience of God. The person we discuss is not the only person who will answer to the Lord. Neither are we exempt because we believe our motives are better or our failures are smaller.

When we judge harshly, we can create a community in which people hide their struggles rather than seek help. When every mistake becomes a permanent label, repentance has little room to breathe. When private information becomes public conversation, people learn to protect their image instead of addressing their character. A healthy church takes sin seriously while making room for truth, responsible accountability, repentance, and repair.

Mercy does not mean calling evil good. It means refusing to add cruelty to correction. Accountability asks what happened, who was affected, what must stop, and what repair is possible. Contempt asks how a person's failure can be used to lower them in the eyes of others. Those purposes are not the same, even when the conversation begins with the same event.

Before we speak about another person's failure, we should ask whether we would accept the same standard applied to our own words and actions. Would we want our motives assumed without a conversation? Would we want our mistakes repeated without context? Would we want someone to tell the story accurately, or add their own conclusions? Such questions can expose the double standard we have learned to call discernment.

Speak as Someone Who Also Needs Grace

James 4 warns against speaking evil of one another and reminds us that God alone is Judge and Lawgiver. This does not cancel our responsibility to confront wrongdoing. It reminds us that we are not sovereign over another person's worth, future, or standing before God. Our responsibility is to speak truthfully, act justly, seek appropriate help when needed, and remain open to the correction we ourselves require.

When a difficult conversation is necessary, begin with the facts you can support. State the concern without adding motives you cannot prove. Choose a person who has a legitimate role in helping. Be willing to listen to information that may correct your understanding. If you have spoken unfairly, acknowledge it plainly and take reasonable steps to correct the impression you helped create.

There will be moments when truth is painful and accountability is unavoidable. Our calling is not to make every truth comfortable. It is to make sure our own conduct is governed by truth rather than pride. We can be firm without being cruel, clear without being careless, and courageous without turning another person's struggle into a source of personal importance.

Before you measure another person's failure, let God examine the measure in your own hand.

Personal Application

Recall a recent moment when you felt certain about another person's motives. Write down what you directly observed, then separate it from what you assumed. Ask God to show you whether hurt, frustration, fear, or pride influenced your conclusion.

Choose one practical response. You may need to ask a clarifying question, address a concern with the appropriate person, correct an assumption you repeated, or acknowledge a double standard in your own behavior. Do not use self examination to deny genuine harm. Use it to make your response truthful, fair, and accountable before God.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, keep me from using one measure for others and another for myself. Search my heart when I am quick to accuse and slow to examine my own conduct. Teach me to distinguish what I know from what I assume, and give me courage to address wrongdoing without contempt. Protect those who need help, guide me toward wise and responsible action, and make me willing to receive correction when I am wrong. Let my words reflect truth, humility, and mercy. In the name of Jesus, amen.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Where are you most tempted to give yourself context while giving someone else a label?
  2. How can you distinguish an observed action from an assumption about someone's motive?
  3. Why does self examination strengthen rather than weaken responsible accountability?
  4. How can spiritual language be used to disguise contempt or personal resentment?
  5. What would it look like to apply the same standard of honesty to your own speech that you expect from others?
