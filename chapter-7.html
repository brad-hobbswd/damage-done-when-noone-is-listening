Part 3 · Chapter 7

Not Every Prayer Request Is an Act of Love

✧

Protecting privacy and sharing only what is appropriate, necessary, and constructive.

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scriptures: Matthew 6:5–6; Matthew 18:15–17; 1 Corinthians 13:1–7; Philippians 2:3–4; Ephesians 4:29

Prayer Should Turn Our Hearts Toward God, Not Turn People Into Topics

Prayer is a sacred privilege. It allows us to bring our needs before God, intercede for others, seek wisdom, and ask for strength when someone is struggling. In the church, prayer requests can help people receive support they might not be able to find alone. Yet something intended to serve a person can become an occasion to expose them when we share more than the situation requires.

Matthew 6:5–6

And when thou prayest, thou shalt not be as the hypocrites are: for they love to pray standing in the synagogues and in the corners of the streets, that they may be seen of men. Verily I say unto you, They have their reward. But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly.

A request may begin with sincere concern. We may genuinely want someone to be healed, restored, protected, or strengthened. But sincerity alone does not determine whether everything we say is appropriate. We can mean well and still reveal private information, repeat details that are not ours to share, or encourage listeners to form judgments about a person whose circumstances they do not understand.

The question is not whether we are allowed to pray for one another. Scripture calls believers to care for one another. The question is whether our words are serving that care or quietly turning another person's difficulty into a subject of conversation.

A prayer request should help us carry a burden before God, not give us permission to carry someone's private life from person to person.

Concern Does Not Give Us Unlimited Permission

Knowing something about another person does not automatically give us the right to share it. A friend may have trusted us with a painful struggle. A family may be facing a crisis. Someone may be receiving pastoral support for a private matter. Even if we believe prayer would help, we should consider what information is necessary and whether the person has agreed to have it shared.

Before passing along details, ask whether the person has given permission, whether the listeners need to know the information, and whether the same prayer can be offered without identifying the person or describing private circumstances. Often we can say, “Please pray for someone who is facing a difficult season and needs strength and wisdom,” without telling a story that belongs to someone else.

There are circumstances when more detail is needed. A trusted pastor, safeguarding lead, counselor, medical professional, or appropriate authority may need specific information to provide care or respond to a serious concern. In that case, share relevant facts with the people who have a legitimate responsibility to act. Privacy is important, but it should not be used to prevent necessary help or conceal harm.

Discretion is not the same as secrecy at any cost. It is the wisdom to understand what should be shared, with whom, for what purpose, and through which channel.

When the Request Becomes a Story

Notice the difference between asking for prayer and telling a story about someone. A prayer request may begin with a simple need, but the speaker adds details about past failures, private conflicts, suspected motives, or embarrassing behavior. The conversation moves beyond what is needed to pray or provide help. Listeners are given material to discuss, and the person in need becomes an object of attention.

Sometimes this shift is subtle. We may say, “I am not trying to gossip, but you need to know what happened.” We may insist that we are only sharing because we care. Yet the content and direction of the conversation matter more than the label we give it. Are we seeking a constructive response, or are we inviting others to agree with our assessment? Are we protecting the person's dignity, or making their failure easier to remember than their humanity?

First Corinthians 13 reminds us that spiritual expression without love is empty. Love does not require us to pretend that wrongdoing never happened, but it does require us to resist using another person's vulnerability for attention, influence, or emotional satisfaction. If the details do not help us pray wisely, provide appropriate support, or address a real concern, we should consider leaving them unspoken.

Prayer Is Not a Cover for Curiosity

Curiosity often disguises itself as concern. We want to know what happened, why it happened, what the person said, what the pastor plans to do, or whether the situation confirms what we already suspected. We may ask questions that sound compassionate while gathering details that do not belong to us.

A useful test is to ask what we intend to do with the information. If we receive an answer, will it help us provide practical support, pray with understanding, or connect the person with someone who can help? Or will it simply satisfy our interest and give us something else to discuss? Not every question needs to be asked, and not every detail needs to be known.

Philippians 2:3–4 calls believers away from selfish ambition and toward humility, directing us to consider the interests of others. That principle changes the way we handle personal information. Instead of asking how much we can share, we ask what will serve the person. Instead of treating access to a private story as a privilege, we treat it as a responsibility.

Sometimes the most loving response is to say less, pray faithfully, offer practical help, and allow the person to decide what they are ready to share.

Choose the Right Audience and the Right Purpose

Not every person in a congregation needs to know every concern. A prayer group may need a brief request. A pastor may need a fuller account to provide support. A safeguarding professional or civil authority may need specific facts when a person is at risk or serious misconduct has been reported. The appropriate audience depends on the need and the responsibility involved.

When possible and safe, ask the person how they would like the request shared. Respect their answer and do not assume that being part of the same church gives you unrestricted access to their personal circumstances. If the person cannot consent, or if immediate safety concerns make delay dangerous, seek guidance from the appropriate people and share only what is needed to protect them or respond responsibly.

There are times when a person may ask us to keep something confidential, but that request cannot require us to conceal abuse, threats, exploitation, or imminent danger. We should explain the limits of confidentiality when necessary and seek appropriate help. Reporting a genuine concern to someone responsible for responding is not the same as spreading gossip among people who cannot help.

The purpose should remain clear: prayer, care, protection, truth, or responsible action. If the conversation has no constructive destination, pause before continuing it.

Speak About People as People

When someone is struggling, it is easy for their difficulty to become the main thing we remember about them. We may reduce a whole life to one failure, one accusation, one family crisis, or one season of weakness. But the person we discuss has dignity beyond the information we possess. They are not a story for us to manage, a warning for us to repeat, or an example to use without care.

Ephesians 4:29

Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers.

Love does not erase accountability. It shapes the way accountability is pursued. We can speak truthfully about a concern without mocking, exaggerating, or turning it into entertainment. We can acknowledge uncertainty instead of presenting assumptions as facts. We can protect someone's privacy while still acting when safety or justice requires it.

That standard asks more of us than avoiding obviously cruel words. It asks whether our speech is fitting, whether it serves a good purpose, and whether the people listening are being helped toward a responsible response.

Let Prayer Change the Way You Speak

Prayer should make us more attentive to God's character and more responsible toward the people we mention. When we bring someone before the Lord, we should be willing to ask Him to correct our own motives as well as meet their need. We may need to repent of curiosity, ask forgiveness for sharing too much, or correct information that has already spread.

If you realize that you have used a prayer request to expose someone, do not hide behind your original intention. Stop repeating the details. Where appropriate, acknowledge the breach of trust, correct any false impression you helped create, and make a different choice next time. If a serious concern needs to be addressed, take it to the people equipped to respond rather than circulating it through informal conversations.

The church should be a place where people can seek help without fearing that their vulnerability will become public conversation. That kind of community does not happen because everyone knows how to use religious language. It grows when believers practice discretion, truthful speech, humility, and love consistently.

Before sharing someone's burden, make sure your words help carry it rather than make it heavier.

Personal Application

Think of a recent prayer request you shared or heard. Did the information shared serve a clear purpose? Was the audience appropriate? Could the same prayer have been offered with fewer personal details? If you were the person being discussed, would you consider the conversation respectful and helpful?

Choose one practical commitment for the coming week. You might ask permission before sharing a personal request, keep identifying details private, redirect a conversation that becomes speculation, or take a serious concern to the proper person instead of discussing it broadly. If you have already shared information carelessly, consider what correction or apology is appropriate. Do not use privacy as a reason to ignore abuse or misconduct that needs responsible reporting.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, purify my motives when I speak about the needs and struggles of others. Teach me to pray with compassion, respect privacy, and share only what is appropriate and necessary. Keep curiosity, resentment, and the desire for agreement from disguising themselves as concern. Give me wisdom to know when to speak, when to remain silent, and when a serious matter must be taken to someone able to help. If my words have exposed or harmed another person, give me humility to take responsibility and make things right where possible. Let my speech reflect Your truth, mercy, and righteousness. In Jesus' name, amen.

Questions for Reflection

  1. What is the difference between sharing a prayer request and turning another person's struggle into a story?
  2. What questions should you ask before sharing private information about someone else?
  3. How can you tell whether the person receiving the information has a legitimate reason to know it?
  4. Why is responsible reporting of abuse or misconduct different from gossip?
  5. What change will help you protect people's dignity when you pray and speak about them?
