Part 3 · Chapter 9

When Spiritual Language Hides the Heart

✧

Examining when words such as concern, discernment, and accountability conceal resentment or control.

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scriptures: Jeremiah 17:9–10; Psalm 139:23–24; Matthew 7:1–5; James 3:13–18; 1 Peter 3:8–12

The Words We Choose Do Not Always Reveal the Motive We Claim

Some of the most difficult conversations to examine are the ones that sound spiritually responsible. We may say that we are concerned, that we are exercising discernment, that someone needs accountability, or that we are trying to protect the church. Those concerns can be genuine and necessary. Yet the words we use to describe a conversation do not automatically prove that our motives are pure.

Concern can become a way to keep discussing a person after there is no constructive purpose left. Discernment can become a name for suspicion when we treat our impressions as facts. Accountability can become a demand for agreement when we want to control how others see a person or situation. The language may sound righteous, but we must still examine the substance and direction of what we are saying.

This is not a call to assume the worst about ourselves or others. It is a call to be honest. We cannot judge every hidden motive in another person's heart, and we should not present speculation as spiritual insight. But we can examine our own intentions, consider the fruit of our words, and ask whether our conduct agrees with the truth we profess.

A spiritual explanation for our words does not remove our responsibility to examine why we speak them.

Concern Must Lead Somewhere Constructive

Genuine concern looks beyond the satisfaction of talking about a problem. It asks what action could help, who is equipped to respond, and what information is necessary. If a person is struggling, concern may lead us to pray, offer practical support, speak with them when appropriate, or seek guidance from someone who can help. If someone is at risk, concern may require prompt reporting through a responsible channel.

But when a conversation repeatedly circles around someone's failures without moving toward prayer, support, correction, protection, or another constructive action, we should pause. Are we still trying to help, or have we become attached to discussing the person? Do we want the problem resolved, or do we want others to continue seeing the person through the story we have told?

Concern is not measured by how many details we can recount or how intensely we speak. It is measured, in part, by whether our response respects truth, dignity, and the real need before us. Sometimes concern means taking action. Sometimes it means keeping a confidence. Sometimes it means admitting that we are not the right person to handle the matter.

Love does not require us to ignore wrongdoing. It requires us to seek a response that addresses wrongdoing without turning a person's pain into a source of attention or influence.

Discernment Is Not Permission to Assume

Discernment should help us recognize what is true and respond wisely. It should not give us permission to claim that we know why someone acted, what they secretly intended, or what must be happening beyond the evidence available to us. We may observe an action, hear an explanation, and notice a pattern. But an observation is not the same as a complete understanding of another person's heart.

Matthew 7:1–5 warns against judging others while ignoring our own need for correction. Jesus does not forbid every form of moral discernment. The passage confronts hypocrisy: the person who focuses on another's fault while refusing to examine their own. Before we announce what we believe is wrong with someone else, we must be willing to ask what we may be missing and what God needs to correct in us.

When a matter is uncertain, speak with appropriate care. Identify what you directly observed, distinguish it from what you heard from someone else, and acknowledge what remains unknown. Do not transform suspicion into certainty through repetition. Saying that we have discernment does not make an unsupported claim reliable.

Humility does not require us to ignore patterns or abandon sound judgment. It requires us to keep our conclusions proportionate to the facts and to remain willing to be corrected when new information becomes available.

Accountability Is Not Control

Accountability has a legitimate place in Christian life. Believers are called to encourage one another, correct wrongdoing, and pursue truth. Leaders must be accountable for the authority entrusted to them, and serious concerns must be addressed rather than concealed. But accountability is not a spiritual word for making another person submit to our preferences or accept our version of events.

Control often appears when we decide that only our interpretation is acceptable, when questions are treated as rebellion, or when people are pressured to agree before the facts have been examined. It may appear when someone uses a position, a claimed revelation, or an appeal to unity to prevent others from raising a legitimate concern. In such circumstances, spiritual language can become a shield against accountability rather than a path toward it.

Healthy accountability is guided by truth, fairness, appropriate process, and a willingness for everyone involved to be examined. It does not depend on humiliation or rumor. It allows concerns to be assessed by people who have a legitimate responsibility to respond, and it recognizes that serious allegations may require safeguarding professionals or civil authorities rather than informal church conversations alone.

We should also examine ourselves when we are the person asking for accountability. Are we seeking a fair resolution, or are we trying to force an apology, control someone's relationships, or punish them through public shame? The desire to be right can become so powerful that we stop caring whether our methods are right.

When Righteous Language Conceals Resentment

Resentment can survive beneath religious language. We may say that we are warning others while actually wanting a person to lose credibility. We may say that we are protecting the church while trying to ensure that no one listens to someone who disagrees with us. We may describe a conversation as correction while enjoying the opportunity to recount another person's failure.

We cannot establish another person's hidden motive merely because their words trouble us. But we can recognize warning signs in our own conduct. Do we feel disappointed when a conflict is resolved because the story can no longer be discussed? Do we repeat details that do not help the situation? Do we resist corrections to our own account? Do we apply standards to others that we would not accept for ourselves?

Jeremiah reminds us that the heart can deceive us, so we must invite God to examine what we cannot fully see in ourselves:

Jeremiah 17:9–10

The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? I the LORD search the heart, I try the reins, even to give every man according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings.

Psalm 139:23–24 gives us a prayerful response: ask God to search us, reveal what is wrong, and lead us in the right way. Such prayer requires more than asking God to confirm our opinion of another person. It means giving Him permission to expose our resentment, pride, fear, and desire for control.

Psalm 139:23–24

Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting.

When God reveals a wrong motive, the answer is not to rename it with more acceptable language. The answer is confession, repentance, and a changed response.

Truth Must Be Spoken With Both Courage and Care

Some people hear teaching about gossip and conclude that every difficult matter should remain private. That is not the teaching of Scripture. Abuse, threats, exploitation, serious misconduct, and situations involving danger may require prompt disclosure to people responsible for protection and action. A person should not be pressured to remain silent to protect a leader's reputation, preserve the appearance of unity, or avoid making the church uncomfortable.

At the same time, the seriousness of a concern does not justify careless exaggeration or spreading unverified claims. Responsible reporting focuses on relevant facts, appropriate recipients, and the steps needed to address harm. It does not turn an allegation into entertainment or invite people without a role in the matter to take sides.

We must learn to hold these responsibilities together. Do not use privacy to conceal harm, and do not use concern to justify unnecessary exposure. Do not mistake every question for rebellion, and do not mistake every confident accusation for truth. Seek appropriate help, respect proper processes, and remain honest about what you know and what you do not know.

Speaking truthfully may require courage. Speaking responsibly requires restraint. Christian maturity calls us to practice both.

Let the Word Examine Your Own Heart First

The purpose of this chapter is not to give us new language for diagnosing everyone around us. It is to make us more willing to submit our own speech and motives to the Word of God. We can become so skilled at identifying spiritual language used by others that we fail to notice the same patterns in ourselves.

Before repeating a concern, ask whether the information is accurate, whether the audience is appropriate, and whether the conversation has a constructive purpose. Before claiming discernment, ask what you actually know. Before demanding accountability, ask whether you are willing to be accountable for your own words and actions. Before speaking about unity, ask whether you are pursuing peace grounded in truth or merely trying to prevent disagreement.

When you discover that resentment has shaped your words, stop feeding it. Correct what you have misrepresented. Apologize where appropriate. Respect boundaries. If a serious concern requires action, take it to the proper people. Do not demand that others trust your motives simply because you use spiritual language. Let your actions demonstrate the change you claim to seek.

The heart is not corrected by finding a better spiritual label for its motives. It is corrected when we submit those motives to God and choose a truthful, humble, and responsible response.

Personal Application

Think about a conversation in which you used words such as concern, discernment, accountability, or protection. What did you hope would happen as a result of that conversation? Did your words move toward a constructive response, or did they invite others to adopt your opinion? Did you distinguish facts from assumptions and allow room for correction?

Choose one action that reflects honest self examination. You may need to stop repeating a story, correct an unsupported claim, acknowledge resentment, apologize for using spiritual language to justify a hurtful response, or bring a genuine concern to someone equipped to address it. Focus on what is yours to change, and do not use this exercise to dismiss legitimate harm or silence someone seeking help.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, search my heart and reveal the motives I may be hiding behind respectable words. Keep me from calling resentment concern, suspicion discernment, or control accountability. Teach me to speak truthfully, listen humbly, and act with courage when someone needs protection. Give me wisdom to distinguish facts from assumptions and to bring serious concerns to the right people. When my motives are wrong, help me confess them, correct the harm I have caused, and walk in a different spirit. Let my words reflect Your truth, holiness, and love. In Jesus' name, amen.

Questions for Reflection

  1. How can spiritual language make a conversation sound righteous without proving that its motive is right?
  2. What is the difference between discernment and treating assumptions as facts?
  3. How can accountability become an attempt to control another person's response or reputation?
  4. Why must responsible reporting of serious harm remain distinct from gossip?
  5. What motive or speech habit do you need to bring honestly before God?
