Part 3 · Chapter 8

The Gift Does Not Excuse the Character

✧

Why spiritual gifts and public ministry must be accompanied by godly character and accountability.

By Bradley Hobbs

Foundational Scriptures: 1 Corinthians 13:1–7; Galatians 5:22–23; Matthew 7:15–20; 1 Corinthians 14:26–33, 40; James 3:13–18

A Spiritual Gift Is Not a Substitute for a Godly Life

God gives gifts to serve His purpose and strengthen His people. In the church, spiritual gifts and public ministry can encourage faith, bring instruction, and help meet real needs. But the presence of a gift does not mean every attitude, decision, or word of the person exercising it is beyond question. A gift may draw attention, but character reveals how a person is allowing God to shape their life.

Paul makes this distinction unmistakable in 1 Corinthians 13. A person might speak with extraordinary ability, understand mysteries, exercise great faith, or give generously, yet without love those actions amount to nothing before God. Spiritual expression is not permission to neglect the very character that should guide it. A powerful moment in ministry cannot make contempt, dishonesty, cruelty, or refusal of correction acceptable.

This truth requires humility from everyone, whether they preach from a pulpit, lead worship, teach a class, pray with others, serve quietly, or are still growing in faith. No role exempts us from the need to be corrected by Scripture. We must not measure spiritual maturity only by what a person can do in public. We must also consider the fruit their life produces in ordinary relationships, private conversations, and moments of disagreement.

A gift may be visible in a moment. Character is revealed in the choices we keep making.

The Fruit of the Spirit Must Reach Our Conversations

Paul describes the character the Spirit develops in a believer:

Galatians 5:22–23

But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law.

These qualities are not decorative additions to a spiritual life. They shape how we respond to pressure, how we speak when frustrated, how we treat people who cannot benefit us, and how we act when our judgment is challenged.

Self control must reach the tongue. Gentleness must influence the way we correct. Goodness must govern what we repeat about another person. Faithfulness must shape how we handle confidence. Meekness must make us teachable. Love must keep us from using another person's weakness as a source of influence or entertainment.

None of this means that a believer will never fail. Christian growth includes repentance, correction, and learning to respond differently. The question is not whether we have ever spoken wrongly. The question is whether we acknowledge the failure and allow God to change our conduct, or whether we use our spiritual reputation to avoid the work of repentance.

When a person claims spiritual authority but repeatedly refuses self control, honesty, or accountability, the contradiction deserves attention. We should not use a single mistake to condemn someone's entire life, but neither should we ignore a continuing pattern simply because the person is gifted or influential.

Public Anointing Does Not Cancel Private Responsibility

It is possible to speak well in public and speak carelessly in private. A person may preach about holiness and then ridicule someone after a service. They may teach about forgiveness while keeping resentment alive through repeated conversations. They may lead others in prayer while sharing another person's private struggle without a legitimate reason. The public message and private conduct then tell conflicting stories.

God is not limited to the platform, the pulpit, the altar, or the gathering where others can see us. Our private conduct belongs to Him as surely as our public ministry does. The conversations after the service, the messages sent when emotions are high, and the way we speak about someone who has disappointed us are all part of our discipleship.

Matthew 7:15–20 teaches that a tree is known by its fruit. This is not permission to claim that we know every person's heart or to treat our suspicions as facts. It is a call to take observable patterns seriously. Words and actions over time can reveal whether a ministry is producing humility, truth, love, and righteousness, or whether influence is being used to excuse harmful behavior.

We must apply this principle to ourselves first. It is easy to examine the fruit of a leader we dislike while explaining away our own impatience, sharp speech, or unwillingness to receive correction. Honest discernment begins with a willingness to let the Word examine our own lives.

Accountability Protects the Church and the Minister

Accountability is not the enemy of spiritual authority. Healthy accountability helps keep ministry aligned with Scripture, protects people from preventable harm, and gives leaders a place to receive honest correction. A person who is trusted with influence should be willing to explain decisions, acknowledge mistakes, and accept appropriate oversight. The greater the influence, the greater the responsibility to handle it faithfully.

Accountability should be truthful, fair, and directed toward a constructive purpose. It is not a license for public humiliation, rumor, or retaliation. Concerns should be grounded in facts and brought to people who have a legitimate role in responding. When a matter involves abuse, threats, exploitation, or serious misconduct, the response may require safeguarding procedures, professional support, or civil authorities. A church process must not be used to conceal harm or prevent necessary reporting.

Likewise, a person raising a concern should not be dismissed simply because the person being questioned is gifted, popular, or considered anointed. Spiritual gifts do not make anyone infallible. A concern deserves a responsible assessment based on evidence, appropriate process, and the safety of those affected.

Leaders also need people who will speak honestly and respectfully rather than flatter them or quietly reinforce every decision. Correction offered in truth can protect a ministry from deeper failure. A leader who can receive correction demonstrates strength of character, not weakness of calling.

Do Not Confuse Spiritual Power With Spiritual Maturity

Spiritual power and spiritual maturity should not be treated as identical measures. A person may have a notable ability and still need growth in patience, humility, or compassion. Another person may serve quietly without public recognition while demonstrating remarkable faithfulness and love. The church should honor the gifts God gives, but it must not let visible ability become the only measure of spiritual worth.

James 3:13–18 connects wisdom with conduct. The wisdom from above is described as pure, peaceable, gentle, willing to yield, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality and without hypocrisy. This gives us a practical test for the way we handle disagreement, influence, and information. Does our conduct reflect the character of godly wisdom, or are we using spiritual claims to justify envy, strife, or self importance?

Being willing to yield does not mean abandoning truth or surrendering to manipulation. It means we are teachable and prepared to consider a correction when it is sound. Meekness is not weakness, and accountability is not disloyalty. A church becomes healthier when people can ask sincere questions, raise legitimate concerns, and seek correction without being automatically branded rebellious.

Let the Spirit Govern the Gift and the Person

In 1 Corinthians 14, Paul teaches that spiritual activity in the assembly should build up the church and be conducted in an orderly way. Spiritual expression is not meant to become a platform for self importance or confusion. Gifts are to serve the body, and their exercise should remain consistent with the order and instruction of Scripture.

The same principle applies to our words. We cannot claim that a spiritual gift gives us permission to expose private matters, make accusations without evidence, or speak without restraint. Nor should anyone use a claim of spiritual insight to make another person feel unable to ask questions or seek appropriate help. Every claim and every practice must remain accountable to the Word of God.

For Oneness Apostolic believers, our confession of the name of Jesus and our desire for the fullness of the Holy Ghost must be accompanied by a life that reflects the Lord we confess. We do not honor Jesus merely by speaking His name or describing spiritual experiences. We honor Him by submitting our conduct to His Word, repenting when we are wrong, and allowing His Spirit to produce the character He requires.

That transformation is ongoing. We should never assume that a moment of spiritual experience means we have finished growing. We remain responsible for the way we treat people, the words we release, and the correction we receive.

When You See a Contradiction, Respond With Wisdom

When someone's public ministry appears inconsistent with their conduct, avoid two opposite mistakes. Do not excuse a harmful pattern because the person is gifted. Do not turn an unverified impression into a public accusation. Take time to distinguish what you directly observed from what you heard secondhand, and separate established facts from your interpretation of motives.

When appropriate and safe, address a concern through a responsible conversation or established process. If direct contact is unsafe or the matter is serious, seek guidance from an appropriate leader, safeguarding professional, or authority. Keep the audience limited to those who need to know, and do not allow a legitimate concern to become material for gossip.

If you are the person being corrected, listen carefully before answering. Ask for specifics, acknowledge what is true, and take the steps needed to change. If the claim is inaccurate, respond with evidence and calm clarity rather than using your position to intimidate the person who raised it. Neither leadership nor accusation removes the need for truthfulness.

Let Character Give Credibility to Your Confession

The purpose of this teaching is not to diminish spiritual gifts. It is to restore their proper place within a life governed by God. Gifts should serve people, build up the church, and point beyond the individual to the One who gives them. Character should make that service safer, more trustworthy, and more consistent with the gospel we proclaim.

We should desire spiritual gifts, but we must also welcome the Lord's correction. We should value public ministry, but never use it as evidence that our private conduct needs no examination. We should honor leaders, while remembering that every believer remains accountable to Scripture and that serious harm must be addressed responsibly.

The gift may draw attention to what God can do through a person. Godly character shows whether that person is willing to let Him govern the life behind the gift.

Personal Application

Consider how you respond when someone is gifted, admired, or trusted with ministry. Do you assume that public spiritual ability means their conduct should not be questioned? Do you examine your own character as seriously as you examine another person's? When you are corrected, do you listen for truth, or do you rely on your role, experience, or reputation to end the conversation?

Choose one quality from Galatians 5:22–23 to practice intentionally this week. Identify a specific situation in which it should shape your words or actions. If you hold a leadership or ministry role, consider what healthy accountability looks like in your setting. If you witness serious misconduct, seek appropriate help rather than spreading unverified claims or remaining silent to protect someone's reputation.

Prayer

Lord Jesus, let Your Spirit govern both my gifts and my character. Deliver me from pride, self importance, and the temptation to use spiritual experience as a defense against correction. Grow Your fruit in my life so that my words and actions reflect Your truth, love, patience, and holiness. Help me honor the gifts You give without excusing harmful conduct, and help me pursue accountability with fairness, courage, and wisdom. Teach me to receive correction humbly and to speak truthfully when someone needs help. Let my life honor the name of Jesus in public and in private. Amen.

Questions for Reflection

  1. Why does 1 Corinthians 13 teach that spiritual gifts without love are insufficient?
  2. How should the fruit of the Spirit shape the way believers speak and respond to correction?
  3. Why is it dangerous to treat public ministry or spiritual gifts as proof that a person is beyond accountability?
  4. How can a church address serious concerns without relying on gossip, intimidation, or concealment?
  5. What specific step can you take to strengthen godly character alongside your spiritual service?
